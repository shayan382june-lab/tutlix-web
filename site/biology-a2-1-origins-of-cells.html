⚡ A2.1 in 60 seconds

  1. Early Earth had no free oxygen, strong UV, lightning and volcanoes: conditions where simple carbon compounds could form.
  2. A cell is the smallest unit of self-sustaining life; viruses are not cells.
  3. Making a cell needs catalysis, self-replication, self-assembly and compartmentalisation.
  4. Miller–Urey made amino acids from simple gases + sparks; fatty acids form vesicles on their own.
  5. RNA can store information and catalyse reactions, so it was probably the first genetic material.
  6. All life descends from LUCA, which probably lived at hydrothermal vents around 4 billion years ago.
A2.1.1

Conditions on early Earth

Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago. Conditions then were very different from today, and allowed simple carbon compounds to form without living things (pre-biotic chemistry).

  • Atmosphere: likely methane, ammonia, hydrogen, water vapour and carbon dioxide, with little or no free oxygen. Without oxygen, newly formed carbon compounds were not broken down by oxidation.
  • No ozone layer, so strong UV radiation reached the surface, providing energy for reactions.
  • Lightning, volcanoes and high temperatures provided more energy.

A2.1.2

Cells: the smallest units of life

A cell is the smallest unit of self-sustaining life. On its own, it can carry out all the functions of life: metabolism, growth, reproduction, response, homeostasis, nutrition and excretion.

  • Viruses are not cells. They have no metabolism of their own and can only reproduce inside a host cell, so they are usually classed as non-living.
  • The boundary between living and non-living is useful to discuss: there is no single feature that everyone agrees on.

A2.1.4

Miller–Urey: making the molecules of life

In 1952–53, Stanley Miller and Harold Urey tested whether carbon compounds could form under early-Earth conditions. Press Run to try it.

Day 0

The flask is ready: water to boil, and a mixture of methane, ammonia and hydrogen.

  • Boiling water = oceans · gas mixture = early atmosphere · electric sparks = lightning · condenser = rain.
  • After about a week, the water contained amino acids and other carbon compounds.
  • Conclusion: organic molecules can form from inorganic ones under early-Earth conditions.
⚠️ Limitations to mention

Scientists now think the early atmosphere was probably less reducing than Miller assumed, and making amino acids is a long way from making a cell: polymers, replication and membranes still need explaining.

A2.1.5

Vesicles: the first compartments

Fatty acids have a hydrophilic head and a hydrophobic tail. In water they coalesce on their own into spherical bilayers, called vesicles, with tails hidden inside and heads facing water. Press Mix to watch.

This self-assembly gives a simple membrane, solving the problem of compartmentalisation. Vesicles could trap RNA and other molecules inside them, forming protocells.

A2.1.6

RNA: the first genetic material

Today, DNA stores information and proteins (enzymes) catalyse reactions, but each needs the other to be made. The RNA world hypothesis solves this chicken-and-egg problem: RNA can do both jobs.

  • Information: RNA's base sequence stores information and can be self-replicated by complementary base pairing.
  • Catalysis: some RNA molecules, called ribozymes, act as enzymes. Even today, the part of the ribosome that joins amino acids is RNA.
A2.1.7

LUCA: the last universal common ancestor

All living organisms are thought to descend from a single population of cells, the last universal common ancestor (LUCA). Other early life forms existed, but they died out without descendants.

  • Universal genetic code: the same codons code for the same amino acids in all organisms.
  • Shared genes: hundreds of genes are found in all domains of life, so they were probably inherited from LUCA.
  • Shared core biochemistry: the same 20 amino acids, DNA and RNA, ATP and ribosomes.
A2.1.8

Dating the first cells and LUCA

Scientists use several approaches, and their estimates differ, so dates are approximate:

  • Fossils: e.g. stromatolites, layered rocks built by microbes, about 3.5 billion years old, and microfossils in ancient rocks.
  • Isotope ratios: living things take up more carbon-12 than carbon-13, so carbon enriched in ¹²C in old rocks is a chemical sign of past life.
  • Molecular clock: comparing gene sequences of living organisms and estimating how long the differences took to build up.

A2.1.9

Did LUCA live at hydrothermal vents?

Hydrothermal vents are cracks in the deep-sea floor where hot, mineral-rich water gushes out. Several lines of evidence suggest LUCA evolved near them.

  • Fossil evidence: possible fossilised microbes have been found in ancient vent deposits in Quebec, Canada, at least 3.77 billion years old.
  • Genetic evidence: genes thought to have been in LUCA suggest it was anaerobic, tolerated high temperatures and used hydrogen and carbon dioxide, which matches vent conditions.
  • Vents provide energy (chemical gradients) and raw materials, and are protected from UV radiation by the deep water.
